# Section 1 — Macroeconomic Conditions (1998–2007)

## The Soil in Which the Crisis Grew

> "There has been a substantial decline in long-term interest rates during the past year, a development that is clearly at odds with conventional wisdom… For the moment, the broadly unanticipated behavior of world bond markets remains a conundrum."
> — Alan Greenspan, Senate Banking Committee testimony, February 16, 2005

The 2008 crisis was a credit crisis. Credit crises are end-stage events; they are the symptom of a much longer accumulation. To understand how a relatively contained subprime mortgage market — at its peak roughly $1.3 trillion of originations against a $14 trillion U.S. economy — could threaten the solvency of the global banking system, one must first understand the macroeconomic environment that pre-loaded the system with leverage, channeled the world's savings into U.S. mortgage credit, and convinced a generation of risk officers that housing was, structurally, the safest large asset class on Earth.

This section establishes the macro substrate. Sections 2 through 6 then trace how that substrate was processed by the origination, securitization, and derivative machinery into the explosive product that detonated in September 2008.

---

## 1.1 The Post-Dotcom Monetary Regime

### Intuitive view

After the technology stock collapse of 2000–2002 and the September 11, 2001 attacks, the Federal Reserve cut the federal funds rate aggressively — from 6.50% in late 2000 to 1.00% by June 2003 — and held it at 1% for a full year. In real terms (adjusted for inflation), the policy rate was negative for much of 2002–2005. Money was, by any historical standard, extraordinarily cheap.

When the price of credit falls, the demand for credit rises. The most credit-sensitive sectors in any economy are housing and durable goods, because their purchase is typically debt-financed. Cheap credit translates almost mechanically into higher house prices.

### Technical view

The Federal Reserve sets the *federal funds target rate*, which anchors the overnight rate banks charge each other for reserves. This rate transmits to longer-dated interest rates through expectations of future short rates, plus a term premium. Mortgage rates are most closely tied to the 10-year Treasury yield, which is the discounted value of expected short rates over a decade plus a term premium and convexity adjustment.

From mid-2003 onward, the Federal Reserve raised the funds rate 17 consecutive times, from 1.00% to 5.25%, but the 10-year yield barely budged. Greenspan called this the "conundrum." Ben Bernanke, then a Fed Governor, offered the most influential explanation: a *global savings glut*. With short rates rising and long rates anchored, the yield curve flattened and eventually inverted in 2006 — a classical recession signal that was, this time, drowned out by competing narratives.

Crucially, mortgage rates remained low. The conventional 30-year fixed mortgage rate, which had been above 8% in 2000, averaged 5.84% in 2003 and was still 6.41% in mid-2006 despite a tripling of the policy rate. *Mortgage credit was effectively delinked from short-rate policy* — a fact that has both monetary and structural causes detailed below.

### Systemic view

Three deep changes in monetary regime mattered:

1. **The "Greenspan put."** Markets came to expect that the Fed would cut rates aggressively after any significant asset price decline (1987, 1998 LTCM, 2001 dotcom). This implicit insurance lowered the perceived risk of leveraged risk-taking. Volatility became a buyable asset because the central bank had, in effect, written a free out-of-the-money put.

2. **Inflation targeting and the Great Moderation.** The 1990s and early 2000s exhibited the lowest macro volatility in a half-century. Bernanke's 2004 "Great Moderation" speech codified the view that improved monetary policy had structurally reduced cyclical risk. Lower realized volatility, fed into Value-at-Risk (VaR) models, mechanically lowered banks' regulatory capital requirements and increased their permitted balance-sheet leverage. The very stability of the system made it more fragile — a dynamic Hyman Minsky had described decades earlier.

3. **Long rates detached from short rates.** Because mortgages, MBS, and the leveraged trades built on them are priced off long rates, not short rates, the Fed's tightening cycle did not cool the housing market until late, and even then through deteriorating credit quality rather than higher financing costs. Monetary policy's transmission channel into housing had been re-plumbed by financial innovation and external demand.

## 1.2 The Global Savings Glut

### Intuitive view

A "savings glut" is the situation in which the world generates more savings than it has productive domestic investment opportunities. The excess savings have to be deployed somewhere. In the early 2000s, China, oil exporters (Saudi Arabia, Russia, the Gulf states), Japan, Germany, and several East Asian economies ran large current account surpluses. The mirror image was the U.S. current account deficit, which peaked at roughly **−6.0% of GDP in 2006** — the largest in U.S. history outside of wartime.

Mechanically: foreign exporters earned dollars from selling goods to U.S. consumers. They did not spend those dollars on U.S. goods; they recycled them into U.S. financial assets. The most natural assets to buy were the safest dollar instruments — U.S. Treasuries and agency mortgage debt.

### Technical view

Between 2003 and 2007, foreign official holdings of U.S. Treasuries grew from approximately $1.1 trillion to $2.4 trillion. Foreign holdings of agency debt (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac, Ginnie Mae securities) grew from roughly $0.5 trillion to nearly $1.5 trillion. China's foreign exchange reserves rose from $403 billion in 2003 to $1.95 trillion by end-2008; a large fraction was held in dollar-denominated fixed income.

This demand had two effects:

1. **It compressed Treasury and agency MBS yields.** The mortgage rate available to a Florida homebuyer in 2005 was being set, in part, by the People's Bank of China's currency intervention policy.

2. **It created an enormous, price-insensitive bid for "safe" dollar assets.** Sovereign reserve managers were not yield-seeking; they were dollar-warehousing. Their behavior was therefore not corrective when prices misaligned with fundamentals — they bought more as yields fell, because the alternative was to allow their currencies to appreciate against the dollar.

### Systemic view

The savings glut is the missing variable in popular accounts of the crisis. Domestic monetary policy alone cannot explain why long-term mortgage rates remained low while short rates rose; it cannot explain why credit spreads compressed simultaneously across mortgages, corporate bonds, and emerging market debt; and it cannot explain why the appetite for AAA-rated structured product continued to grow even as collateral quality deteriorated.

The savings glut converted the U.S. housing market into a *sink* for global excess savings. Once that sink existed, every layer of the U.S. financial system — originators, securitizers, rating agencies, banks — had a strong commercial incentive to manufacture more "safe-rated" mortgage product to satisfy the demand. The supply curve for AAA paper began to respond to the demand curve, and the easiest way to manufacture more AAA was to engineer it out of progressively worse underlying loans.

Two competing interpretations remain debated among serious analysts:

- **Bernanke / Caballero view:** The crisis was substantially exogenous to U.S. policy — the Fed's rates were close to optimal, and global capital flows did the damage. Without the savings glut, the housing boom would have been ordinary.
- **Taylor / BIS view:** The Fed's policy rate was held materially below the rate implied by the Taylor rule from 2002 to 2005, and this — not foreign flows — was the proximate cause of excessive credit creation. The savings glut was a *contributing* factor at most.

Both views have merit. A defensible synthesis is that the two were complementary: U.S. monetary policy created the local conditions and the savings glut created the external amplifier, with each making the other more powerful than it would have been alone.

## 1.3 The Demand for "Safe" Assets

If the global financial system has a single most-important price, it is the price of safety. Pension funds, insurance companies, sovereign wealth funds, money market funds, central bank reserve portfolios, and bank treasuries together command tens of trillions of dollars. By regulation, by mandate, or by internal risk policy, the vast majority of this capital is constrained to hold securities rated AAA or its near-equivalent. The system *cannot function* without a sufficient supply of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA).

By the mid-2000s, three structural pressures collided:

1. **Demand for AAA was rising** — driven by global demographic aging (more pension liabilities, more annuity portfolios), insurance regulation that favored highly-rated assets via the NAIC's Risk-Based Capital framework, Basel I/II treatment of AAA exposure at low risk weights, and the explosion of dollar reserve accumulation outside the United States.

2. **The natural supply of AAA was constrained.** U.S. Treasury issuance was actually shrinking as a share of global financial assets — the federal government had run a small budget surplus as late as 2001. The number of AAA-rated corporate issuers had been falling for decades (in 1980, more than 60 U.S. industrial companies carried AAA; by 2008, fewer than 10).

3. **Securitization promised to manufacture AAA from non-AAA inputs.** This was the killer application of structured finance: by pooling lower-quality loans and tranching the cash flows, financial engineers could produce a senior bond statistically far safer than any individual underlying loan. With sufficient credit enhancement and the right correlation assumptions, *any* pool could yield a AAA-rated senior tranche.

The system therefore had a structural reason to produce mortgage-backed AAA — and to keep producing it even as underlying loan quality deteriorated. Once demand for AAA outstripped the supply of high-quality mortgages, the industry's options narrowed to two: shrink the AAA pipeline (giving up fee revenue), or maintain AAA volume by reducing the quality of inputs while preserving the appearance of senior-tranche safety through more aggressive structuring. The industry, predictably, chose the second path. Section 3 details the mechanics by which this transformation was performed; Section 4 explains how derivatives multiplied the effective supply far beyond what the underlying mortgage market could physically provide.

This dynamic is the single most important systemic fact of the pre-crisis period. The crisis was, in a deep sense, a *AAA shortage* solved by financial engineering.

## 1.4 The Political Economy of Homeownership

### Intuitive view

For roughly seven decades, U.S. federal policy treated the expansion of homeownership as an unalloyed public good. Tax deductibility of mortgage interest (since 1913 in the Revenue Act, expanded after WWII), the GI Bill (1944), the creation of Fannie Mae (1938), the Federal Housing Administration's loan guarantees, the establishment of Freddie Mac (1970), the Community Reinvestment Act (1977), HUD's affordable housing goals for the GSEs (1992, with periodic increases), and the American Dream Downpayment Initiative (2003) all moved in the same direction: more Americans should own homes, and the federal government should help.

Homeownership rates rose from 64.0% in 1994 to a peak of 69.2% in 2004 — a five-percentage-point increase that represented millions of marginal new homeowners, many of whom would not have qualified under prior underwriting standards.

### Technical view

The Government-Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs) — Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac — sat at the structural center of U.S. mortgage finance. Their charters granted them:

- A line of credit at the U.S. Treasury (small, but symbolically powerful);
- Exemption from state and local taxes;
- An implicit federal guarantee on their senior debt — never explicit but universally understood; their senior debt traded at a spread of roughly 25–50 bps over Treasuries.

In exchange, they were required by HUD to direct an increasing share of their purchases to "affordable housing" — broadly, loans to lower-income or underserved borrowers. The affordable housing goals rose from 30% in 1996 to 56% by 2008. To meet them, the GSEs increasingly purchased loans they would not historically have touched: lower FICO scores, higher loan-to-value ratios, less documentation.

The GSEs' total mortgage exposure (loans held and MBS guaranteed) grew from $2.1 trillion in 2000 to over $5.3 trillion by 2007. They were, by any measure, *too large to fail* long before the phrase entered common usage.

### Systemic view

Whether the GSEs caused the crisis is one of the most genuinely contested questions in the literature. Two views:

- **The "GSE-centric" view** (Wallison, AEI; Pinto): GSE affordable-housing mandates drove down underwriting standards across the entire mortgage market. Subprime would not have grown to systemic scale without GSE participation, and the GSEs were the largest single holder of subprime/Alt-A risk on the eve of the crisis.

- **The "private-label-centric" view** (FCIC majority report; Hubbard, Jaffee): The truly toxic loans — pay-option ARMs, no-documentation subprime, hybrid 2/28 ARMs with steep resets — were overwhelmingly originated and securitized by private-label issuers (Countrywide, New Century, IndyMac, Washington Mutual) and Wall Street, not the GSEs. The GSEs' exposure was real but late, defensive, and concentrated in better-quality loans. The data show that the worst loans by vintage and product type sat outside the GSE books.

The honest empirical answer is that both forces operated. The GSEs lowered standards on conforming product and competed with private-label issuers for marginal borrowers, contributing to a system-wide standards race. But the most explosive growth in loan volume and the most aggressive product innovations occurred in the private-label sector, particularly in 2004–2006, after the GSEs had begun to lose market share. The political environment that *enabled* the deterioration of standards — the bipartisan consensus that more homeownership was always better — was the deeper cause; the GSEs were one instrument of that consensus, not its origin.

## 1.5 Housing as an Asset Class

### Intuitive view

For roughly seventy years, U.S. nominal home prices had risen modestly and, in real terms, had been close to flat over long horizons. (Robert Shiller's long-run real home-price index, normalized to 100 in 1890, sat near 110 in 1997 — a real return of about 0.1% per year over a century.) Between 1997 and 2006, that same index more than doubled, reaching roughly 200. *In nine years, the inflation-adjusted price of U.S. housing rose more than it had in the preceding ninety.*

This was not a uniform national phenomenon. The "sand states" — California, Arizona, Nevada, Florida — saw price increases of 150–250%. The Midwest and rural South saw far smaller appreciation. But the loans being underwritten and the securities being built treated the U.S. housing market as a single, diversifiable asset class.

### Technical view

Three structural conditions allowed housing to be treated, for risk-modeling purposes, as a remarkably safe asset:

1. **The historical data set was short and benign.** National housing data with reliable comparable methodology effectively began with the Case-Shiller Index in the late 1980s, and the Office of Federal Housing Enterprise Oversight (OFHEO, predecessor of FHFA) data set went back to 1975. Neither captured an episode of large national real price decline. The Great Depression's housing decline was poorly documented in any modern-comparable series. *Modelers had no nationally-negative housing year in their training data.*

2. **Geographic diversification was treated as a structural risk-reducer.** Pre-crisis MBS and CDO models typically assumed cross-state housing correlations of 0.1–0.3. In reality, when a national monetary impulse drives appreciation everywhere, correlations are far higher; under stress, they converge toward 1.

3. **The "people pay their mortgage" prior.** Decades of low single-digit aggregate mortgage default rates created a deep behavioral prior among credit officers that homeowners would, in nearly all cases, sacrifice other obligations before missing a mortgage payment. This was true under "old" mortgage demographics — high down payments, fixed-rate amortization, long-tenure borrowers with significant equity. It was emphatically not true under the new conditions: high LTV, teaser-rate ARMs, speculative second-home buyers, and "ruthless default" behavior when equity went negative.

### Systemic view

By 2005, U.S. housing was simultaneously:

- the world's largest single asset class by aggregate value (~$22 trillion);
- the largest collateral pool for the global financial system;
- the input to securitization machinery generating tens of trillions of dollars of leveraged derivative exposure; and
- modeled, throughout, as a low-volatility, low-correlation asset.

The error was not that any individual modeler thought housing could never decline. It was that the *combined effect* of those individual errors — each modest in isolation — was a financial system in which a 20% national decline in home prices, an event with a non-trivial historical probability, would be sufficient to render major financial institutions insolvent. The system was engineered for a world that never quite existed.

## 1.6 Yield Compression, the Search for Yield, and Institutional Demand

The mid-2000s exhibited a phenomenon credit analysts call *spread compression*. The yield premium investors demanded for taking credit risk over Treasury risk collapsed across nearly every asset class:

| Spread / Date | January 2002 | June 2007 |
|---------------|-------------:|----------:|
| U.S. Investment-Grade Corporate (vs. UST) | ~180 bps | ~95 bps |
| U.S. High-Yield (vs. UST) | ~860 bps | ~250 bps |
| Emerging Markets Sovereign (EMBI+) | ~720 bps | ~150 bps |
| AAA CMBS | ~80 bps | ~25 bps |
| AAA Subprime ABS | ~70 bps | ~10 bps |
| BBB Subprime ABS | ~280 bps | ~120 bps |

*Indicative levels, sourced from Federal Reserve H.15, ICE BofA index history, and JPMorgan EMBI series.*

Three mechanisms combined to drive this:

1. **The savings glut** (1.2): foreign reserve flows hit the long end of the curve and forced yields lower.
2. **The low volatility regime** (1.1): Sharpe-ratio-targeted investors were systematically pushed up the risk spectrum to maintain return targets, compressing risk premia.
3. **Carry trades**: with short rates near zero in Japan (the original yen carry trade) and modest in the U.S., levered investors borrowed cheap short money and bought higher-yielding long credit. This is mechanically destabilizing: it works until funding markets tighten or asset prices fall, at which point the trade unwinds violently.

For institutional investors with absolute-return mandates — endowments, pension funds, insurance general accounts — yield compression created an existential problem. A pension fund discounting liabilities at 7.5% needs to *earn* 7.5%. When safe assets pay 4.5% and corporate bonds pay 5.4%, that earned yield is unattainable without taking incremental risk somewhere. The structured credit market obligingly provided that incremental risk in the form of products that *appeared* safe (AAA-rated) but yielded 30–80 bps above Treasuries — an apparently free lunch.

This is what Raghuram Rajan, in his prescient 2005 Jackson Hole paper "Has Financial Development Made the World Riskier?", described as the tendency of professional investment managers, evaluated against benchmarks and short performance horizons, to take *tail risk* in exchange for *modest carry* — a trade that wins almost every year and loses catastrophically in the rare year of stress. The pre-crisis financial system was, at its core, an enormous short-volatility, short-correlation, short-tail-risk trade.

## 1.7 The Refinancing Channel and the Consumer Balance Sheet

Cheap mortgage credit did more than fund home purchases. It transformed the home into an ATM. Between 2001 and 2007, U.S. households extracted approximately $2.0 trillion of home equity through cash-out refinancings and home equity lines of credit (HELOCs), according to Federal Reserve studies (Greenspan and Kennedy, FEDS Working Paper 2007-20).

This had several effects:

- **It propped up consumer spending.** Mortgage equity withdrawal funded roughly 2–3 percentage points of GDP-equivalent consumption per year at its peak.
- **It increased aggregate household leverage.** Household debt rose from 95% of disposable personal income in 2000 to 132% in 2007.
- **It systematically reduced the equity cushion in housing.** Loans that had been originated at 80% LTV were refinanced (often into the same property at appreciated value) at 90–95% LTV. The buffer between the loan and the underwater threshold shrank just as the system became more dependent on rising prices to stay solvent.
- **It created a behavioral expectation of continuous appreciation.** Households began to treat home equity as a source of liquid wealth — a regime sustainable only if prices kept rising.

The macro picture by 2006 was a closed loop: low rates → high home prices → equity extraction → consumer spending → strong GDP → continued benign credit data → continued low rates → still higher home prices. The loop was self-reinforcing on the way up and, as Section 6 details, became self-reinforcing in reverse on the way down.

## 1.8 The Rates–Leverage–Housing Feedback Mechanism

The macro picture can be condensed into a single causal diagram:

```
   Low fed funds (2001–2004)
            │
            ▼
   Low long-end yields (savings glut + low term premium)
            │
            ▼
   Low mortgage rates
            │
            ▼
   Higher housing affordability at higher prices
            │
            ▼
   Rising home prices ──► Equity extraction ──► Consumer spending
            │                                          │
            ▼                                          ▼
   Investor demand for housing exposure          Strong macro data
            │                                          │
            ▼                                          ▼
   Origination supply expands ──► Underwriting deteriorates
            │
            ▼
   Mortgage volume grows ──► Securitization volume grows
            │
            ▼
   AAA paper supply grows ──► Global safe-asset demand met
            │
            ▼
   Spreads compress ──► Search for yield intensifies
            │
            ▼
   More leveraged carry into structured credit

   System-wide short-volatility/short-correlation positioning
            │
            ▼
   ╔══════════════════════════════════════════════════════╗
   ║ Latent fragility: small disturbance → large response ║
   ╚══════════════════════════════════════════════════════╝
```

Each step in the chain was, in isolation, defensible. The Fed had real reasons to ease in 2002. Chinese authorities had real reasons to peg the renminbi and recycle dollars. American politicians had real reasons to want broader homeownership. Investors had real reasons to seek yield. Lenders had real reasons to extend credit when collateral values were rising. Banks had real reasons to retain senior tranches that empirically had never defaulted. Rating agencies had real reasons (the empirical default record, plus the absence of nationally negative price years in their data) to rate those tranches AAA.

What no participant could see was the *aggregate position*. The system as a whole was short housing volatility, short correlation, and long leverage — a configuration that has a name in derivatives theory: a *short gamma* portfolio. Such portfolios are characterized by smooth-looking returns until they aren't. The change in P&L is non-linear in the underlying state variable. A move from "good" to "slightly worse" produces a modest loss. A move from "slightly worse" to "stress" produces a catastrophic loss.

That is the macroeconomic state in which the U.S. financial system entered 2007.

## 1.9 Adjacent Conditions Often Overlooked

Several macro factors specifically relevant to the 2008 outcome deserve explicit mention, as they are often glossed in popular accounts:

**1. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (1999)** repealed Glass-Steagall separation between commercial and investment banking. Its direct causal contribution to the crisis is debated — much of the worst risk-taking occurred in pure investment banks (Bear, Lehman) that did not benefit from the repeal, and in pure commercial banks (Washington Mutual, IndyMac) that did not engage in investment banking. But the repeal *enabled* the construction of large universal banks (Citigroup, JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America) whose combined balance sheets concentrated systemic risk and whose failure during the crisis would have been catastrophic — a fact that materially constrained policy options in 2008.

**2. The Commodity Futures Modernization Act (2000)** explicitly exempted OTC derivatives — including credit default swaps — from CFTC regulation. This decision, lobbied for by the dealer banks and supported by Treasury Secretary Summers and Fed Chairman Greenspan, removed the legal basis for clearing requirements, position limits, margin requirements, and trade reporting on the very instruments that would become the central vector of contagion. AIG Financial Products wrote half a trillion dollars of CDS exposure precisely because no regulator was empowered to require it to post collateral against that exposure.

**3. The SEC's 2004 Consolidated Supervised Entities (CSE) program** allowed the five largest U.S. investment banks (Bear, Lehman, Merrill, Goldman, Morgan Stanley) to use internal models to calculate net capital, effectively raising their permitted leverage ratios. Subsequent investigation (SEC Office of Inspector General Report 446-A, 2008) showed that the CSE program failed to adequately supervise model assumptions and that leverage ratios at the major investment banks reached 30:1 to 40:1 by 2007. Three of the five firms collapsed or were forced into mergers within four years of the program's creation.

**4. Basel I's risk-weight structure** assigned a 100% risk weight to whole-loan mortgages but only a 50% risk weight to GSE-guaranteed MBS and a 20% risk weight to AAA-rated private-label MBS — and zero risk weight to OECD sovereign debt and AAA-rated tranches under certain conditions in some interpretations. Banks were thus capital-incentivized to *not* hold mortgages on balance sheet, but to securitize them and either retain the AAA tranche or distribute it. The capital regime did not merely permit securitization; it actively rewarded it relative to the alternative of traditional portfolio lending.

**5. The 1986 Tax Reform Act** eliminated personal interest deductions but preserved mortgage interest deductibility, creating a strong incentive for U.S. households to convert credit card and consumer debt into HELOC debt. By 2005, HELOCs had become a primary vehicle for unsecured consumption financing — collateralized on appreciated home values that, when those values reversed, became unrecoverable.

Each of these adjacent factors was small in isolation but compounded with the rest. The cumulative effect was a financial system whose every important regime — monetary, fiscal, regulatory, supervisory, accounting, capital — pointed in the same direction: toward expanded mortgage credit, expanded securitization, expanded leverage, and reduced friction. There was no major countervailing force. When all of the system's vectors point the same way for long enough, the system arrives somewhere far from its long-run equilibrium and cannot easily return.

## 1.10 Closing Frame for Part I

Section 1 has established that by 2006 the macroeconomic environment exhibited:

- A historically low cost of capital, especially at the long end of the curve, decoupled from short-rate policy by foreign demand;
- A structural global shortage of safe assets that securitization was incentivized to fill;
- A political consensus expanding the homeownership franchise to marginal borrowers;
- A regulatory and capital regime that subsidized securitization relative to portfolio lending;
- A housing market modeled as nationally diversified despite being driven by a single national monetary impulse;
- A consumer balance sheet that had monetized home appreciation into ongoing consumption;
- A professional investor base structurally short volatility, short correlation, and long carry;
- A derivatives market in which the central instrument of credit-risk transfer was explicitly exempted from oversight; and
- A bank balance sheet structure operating at 30:1+ leverage on a collateral base concentrated in real estate.

The next six sections describe how this substrate was processed — from the borrower's loan application through the AIG-FP CDS portfolio — and how the system's failure modes activated in sequence between February 2007 and October 2008. Section 2 begins at the origination point: the borrower, the broker, the lender, and the loan.
