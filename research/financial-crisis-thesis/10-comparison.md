# Section 10 — Systemic Comparison: 2008 vs Today

## Where Has the System Strengthened, Where Has Risk Shifted, and Where Is the System Genuinely New?

> "It is the same river, but never the same water. The patterns repeat at the level of structure; the specifics never do."
> — Composite paraphrase, after Heraclitus

This section organizes the comparison between the pre-2008 financial system and the present system across multiple structural dimensions. For each dimension, the question is the same: has the post-2008 reform architecture *eliminated* the relevant fragility, has it merely *shifted* the fragility to a different part of the system, or has the system developed a *genuinely new* feature whose stress behavior is not yet known?

The disciplined answer for most dimensions is "some combination." Honest comparison requires acknowledging where real progress has been made and where it has not.

---

## 10.1 Leverage

### 10.1.1 The pre-crisis state

In 2007:
- **Major U.S. investment banks**: 30:1 to 40:1 total leverage, with material additional exposure off-balance-sheet through conduits and SIVs.
- **Universal banks**: 20:1 to 25:1 total leverage; lower risk-weighted leverage but with significant off-balance-sheet vehicles consolidating during stress.
- **AIG-FP**: effectively unlimited derivative leverage on a parent company with modest insurance reserve capital.
- **Hedge funds**: typically 3–5x portfolio leverage, but with extreme leverage in specific strategies (subprime CDO bridge financing at 20x+).
- **Households**: mortgage debt to disposable income ratio of 132% — record level.
- **Government**: U.S. federal debt-to-GDP of 64%.

### 10.1.2 The current state

In 2026:
- **Major U.S. banks**: Common Equity Tier 1 ratios of 11–13%; total leverage roughly 10–12:1. Off-balance-sheet exposure substantially reduced; SIVs eliminated; conduits subject to consolidation tests.
- **Foreign G-SIBs**: similar improvement; somewhat higher leverage on average than U.S. peers but well below pre-crisis levels.
- **Investment-bank-style activities**: substantially constrained at deposit-taking institutions by the Volcker Rule and Basel III. Surviving prop activity has migrated to dedicated hedge funds and non-banks.
- **Private credit funds**: typically 0–2x leverage at the fund level, with portfolio companies levered at 5–7x EBITDA. Aggregate effective leverage on the underlying credit exposure is comparable to bank leverage on similar lending pre-crisis, possibly higher.
- **Hedge funds**: leverage levels vary by strategy; aggregate sector leverage modest, but specific strategies (Treasury basis trade, multi-strategy platforms) operate at very high leverage on specific positions.
- **Households**: debt-to-income ~96%; mortgage-specific lower than pre-crisis.
- **Government**: U.S. federal debt-to-GDP ~124% — nearly double pre-crisis.

### 10.1.3 Assessment

- **Genuinely reduced**: leverage at the major banks. This is the most clearly successful element of post-crisis reform. G-SIBs are substantially better capitalized, have higher-quality capital (more common equity vs. hybrid instruments), and face leverage ratio constraints in addition to risk-weighted measures.
- **Shifted, not reduced**: leverage on credit exposures has moved from banks to private credit funds, BDCs, insurance companies, and specialty lenders. The aggregate leverage on U.S. credit may not be substantially lower than pre-crisis; it is held in different vehicles.
- **Increased**: government debt has approximately doubled relative to GDP. This is a different kind of leverage — sovereign rather than financial-sector — but it constrains the policy options for crisis response.

## 10.2 Capitalization of the Banking System

### 10.2.1 Pre-crisis

U.S. G-SIBs had reported Tier 1 capital ratios of 7–9% pre-crisis. Risk weights were lower (Basel I allowed 20% risk weight on AAA-rated securities, including the CDO super-seniors that proved to be the most damaging). Counter-cyclical buffers did not exist. The G-SIB capital surcharge did not exist.

Stress testing did not exist as a regulatory framework. The first U.S. supervisory stress test (the Supervisory Capital Assessment Program, "SCAP") was conducted in May 2009 *during* the crisis, on an ad hoc basis.

### 10.2.2 Current

U.S. G-SIBs in 2026 are subject to:

- **Minimum CET1 ratio**: 4.5% of risk-weighted assets.
- **Capital conservation buffer**: 2.5%.
- **Countercyclical buffer**: 0–2.5% (currently 0% in the U.S.).
- **G-SIB surcharge**: 1–3.5% (JPMorgan at top of range; others 1–2.5%).
- **Stress capital buffer**: based on annual stress testing, varies by bank.
- **Combined effective minimum**: typically 11–13% CET1 for major G-SIBs.

Actual ratios are typically several points above the minimum requirements. The largest U.S. banks (JPMorgan, Bank of America, Citigroup, Wells Fargo) have CET1 ratios of 12–14% as of 2025–2026.

### 10.2.3 Assessment

The capitalization of major banks is dramatically better than pre-crisis. This is the single most successful reform.

Several caveats:

- The improvement is concentrated at G-SIBs and large regional banks. Smaller community banks remain on a lighter framework.
- The held-to-maturity unrealized loss problem demonstrated in 2023 (Section 9.2) shows that book capital ratios can overstate true loss-absorption capacity when rates move significantly.
- Risk weights remain subject to debate. The market-risk capital framework was revised after 2008 (Fundamental Review of the Trading Book) but trading-book positions still rely heavily on model-based capital calculation.
- Internal-models approaches (IRB) for credit risk produce lower risk weights for sophisticated banks. The "model arbitrage" concern is real and has been addressed by output floors in Basel III.endgame implementations.

## 10.3 Asset Quality and Underwriting Discipline

### 10.3.1 Mortgages

Mortgage underwriting (Section 8) is dramatically improved. The QM framework eliminates the worst pre-crisis products. Subprime as a meaningful share of origination volume essentially does not exist. Median origination FICO is 760 vs. 705 pre-crisis; median CLTV is 78% vs. 84%; documentation is near-universal; ARMs are a small share of originations.

### 10.3.2 Leveraged loans

Underwriting in the leveraged loan market is *worse* than pre-crisis on several dimensions:

- Covenant-lite share approaches 90% of new issuance, vs. <20% pre-crisis.
- EBITDA addbacks have become more aggressive.
- "Loose drafting" has produced documentation disputes in distressed situations (J.Crew, PetSmart, Serta Simmons "uptier exchanges").
- Leverage multiples are higher: 6.5–7.5x EBITDA at PE buyouts, vs. ~5x pre-crisis.

The compensating features: leveraged loans are senior secured first-lien, while pre-crisis subprime was lower in the cap structure of housing. Sponsors typically have meaningful equity contribution. Lenders include direct lenders with concentrated positions and relationships, who have stronger workout incentives than bond trustees.

### 10.3.3 Commercial real estate

CRE underwriting in the 2019–2022 vintage was, by some measures, more aggressive than pre-crisis: extended interest-only periods, debt-service-coverage-ratios at the edge of feasibility, valuations supported by very low cap rates that proved unsustainable as rates rose. The current vintage is more conservative but the legacy stock requires refinancing.

### 10.3.4 Consumer credit

Auto subprime underwriting has loosened materially over the post-2010 era. Buy Now Pay Later has emerged with minimal underwriting. Credit card lending has expanded to subprime borrowers more aggressively in recent years.

### 10.3.5 Assessment

Mortgage underwriting is dramatically better than pre-crisis. Underwriting in other categories is similar to or in some cases worse than pre-crisis. The "lesson of 2008" was applied specifically to mortgages and has not been generalized to other credit categories.

## 10.4 Securitization Quality

### 10.4.1 Pre-crisis

Private-label residential MBS, ABS CDOs, and CDO-squareds produced ~$2 trillion of subprime-related issuance in the peak years. Rating agency models were systematically biased toward favorable subordination levels. Disclosure was poor; loan-level data was hard to obtain. Issuer-pays rating model created severe conflicts of interest. Originate-to-distribute dynamics decoupled originator incentives from loan performance.

### 10.4.2 Current

- **Agency MBS**: dominates residential securitization. Credit risk effectively underwritten by the federal government via the GSEs in conservatorship. Underwriting quality discussed above.
- **Private-label RMBS**: small market, concentrated in prime jumbo and high-quality non-QM. The pre-crisis private-label segment essentially does not exist in its old form.
- **CLOs**: large market, but built on different (and better) structural foundations than ABS CDOs.
- **CMBS**: substantially smaller post-crisis than peak. Underwriting quality more mixed; office concentration in legacy 2018–2022 vintages.
- **Asset-backed**: large, healthy markets for auto, credit card, equipment, single-family rental, and student loan ABS. Better disclosure under Regulation AB II. 5% risk retention requirement in effect.

The synthetic CDO market of 2005–2007 — the explicit multiplier of mortgage risk — does not exist in remotely comparable form. Synthetic CLO and credit risk transfer (CRT) structures exist but are simpler, more transparent, and used for risk transfer rather than risk multiplication.

### 10.4.3 Assessment

Securitization has been substantially restructured. The most dangerous pre-crisis features (synthetic CDOs, CDO-squareds, opaque private-label RMBS) are largely absent. CLOs and other surviving structures are better-designed and better-disclosed.

The cost has been a dramatic reduction in private-label securitization volume, with the gap filled by GSE conservatorship. This has the practical effect of substituting federal credit guarantee for private credit assessment in the mortgage market — addressing the immediate fragility of poor underwriting but creating a deeper political-economy dependency.

## 10.5 Derivatives Markets

### 10.5.1 Pre-crisis

OTC derivatives were bilateral, uncleared, with minimal supervisory visibility. The CDS market on subprime ABS and CDO references was the principal channel by which the crisis propagated. AIG-FP, with $440bn of CDS notional, was a single-point-of-failure that came within hours of triggering a chain reaction. Margin and collateral practices were inconsistent; rating-dependent collateral thresholds at AIG-FP allowed massive exposure with zero margin.

### 10.5.2 Current

Title VII of Dodd-Frank fundamentally restructured the derivatives market:

- **Standardized OTC derivatives** must be cleared through CCPs.
- **All swaps** must be reported to swap data repositories.
- **Initial and variation margin** are required for uncleared derivatives.
- **Major swap participants and dealers** are subject to direct CFTC/SEC oversight.
- **Position limits** apply to certain commodity derivatives.

The result: dealer-bilateral CDS exposures comparable to AIG-FP's pre-crisis position essentially cannot be built without producing substantial real-time collateral demands and supervisory visibility.

### 10.5.3 Assessment

Derivatives reform is one of the most successful elements of post-crisis architecture. The specific 2008-style scenario — a single uncleared, unmargined, off-balance-sheet exposure of half a trillion dollars at an insurance subsidiary — cannot recur in the same form.

Caveats:

- The risk has been concentrated at CCPs, whose stress behavior is partially understood but not fully tested at scale.
- A significant fraction of derivatives remain uncleared (customized contracts, certain FX, certain commodity); margin requirements have reduced but not eliminated the bilateral risk.
- Margin procyclicality in stress remains a concern.
- New instruments (crypto derivatives, certain structured products) operate at the boundary of the post-crisis framework.

## 10.6 Funding Markets and the Liquidity Architecture

### 10.6.1 Pre-crisis

The dealer balance sheet was financed by very short-term wholesale funding (overnight repo, commercial paper, prime brokerage cash balances). The maturity mismatch was extreme. The Liquidity Coverage Ratio and Net Stable Funding Ratio did not exist. Tri-party repo operated on a foundation of intraday clearing-bank credit that proved fragile during the Bear and Lehman runs.

### 10.6.2 Current

- **LCR**: large banks must hold sufficient high-quality liquid assets to cover 30 days of stressed outflows.
- **NSFR**: must have stable funding for assets and off-balance-sheet exposures over a 1-year horizon.
- **Tri-party repo reform**: reduced reliance on intraday clearing bank credit; now substantially pre-funded.
- **Money market fund reform**: gates, fees, floating NAV for institutional prime funds, stricter liquidity requirements.
- **Treasury market reforms**: central clearing of Treasury repo (in implementation); ongoing discussion of all-to-all trading.

### 10.6.3 Assessment

Bank liquidity has improved substantially. The LCR/NSFR framework was real and binding; pre-crisis funding mismatches comparable to Bear and Lehman do not exist at the major U.S. and global banks today.

However:

- The wholesale funding fragility has migrated to non-banks. The 2020 COVID stress and the 2023 SVB run demonstrated that non-bank intermediaries, money market funds, and even commercial banks with concentrated uninsured deposits remain vulnerable to runs.
- The Federal Reserve's expanded toolkit (Standing Repo Facility, BTFP, FIMA repo) is the practical substitute for full elimination of funding fragility. The Fed is the lender of last resort to a broader perimeter than pre-crisis.
- Treasury market liquidity has structurally deteriorated, with multiple episodes (October 2014 "flash crash"; March 2020; September 2022 UK gilt spillover; recent issuance-driven volatility). The deepest market in the world is, paradoxically, less resilient than 15 years ago.

## 10.7 Shadow Banking and Non-Bank Intermediation

### 10.7.1 Pre-crisis

Shadow banking — credit intermediation outside the regulated banking system — was a major source of pre-crisis credit growth. SIVs, ABCP conduits, money market funds, and structured investment vehicles channeled approximately $7 trillion of credit at peak, much of it backed by short-term funding with no central bank backstop.

### 10.7.2 Current

The shadow banking universe has been substantially restructured but not shrunk:

- **SIVs**: gone. Killed by the 2007–2008 crisis and not revived.
- **ABCP conduits**: dramatically smaller. The conduit business is mostly captive financing of bank trade receivables now.
- **Money market funds**: same total size, structurally reformed.
- **Private credit**: the dominant new shadow banking category. Larger today than the SIV/ABCP system was at peak.
- **Bond mutual funds and ETFs**: larger than ever; significant liquidity mismatch.
- **Insurance companies in alternative credit**: significant new exposure category, particularly at PE-owned insurers.
- **Hedge funds in credit and Treasury basis trades**: large positions; concentrated.
- **CLOs and structured product**: larger than pre-crisis aggregate structured credit if CDOs are excluded.
- **BDCs and interval funds**: a new retail-distributed shadow banking category.

The FSB's narrow measure of NBFI (the most relevant shadow banking aggregate) was approximately $65 trillion globally in 2024, vs. roughly $30 trillion pre-crisis.

### 10.7.3 Assessment

Shadow banking has not been eliminated. It has been restructured. Some of the most dangerous pre-crisis forms (SIVs, structured ABCP) are gone. Others (private credit, NBFI more broadly) are much larger than pre-crisis. The aggregate scale is, by any measure, larger.

The regulatory perimeter has not effectively followed credit intermediation as it migrated to non-banks. The Federal Reserve's de facto lender-of-last-resort role has expanded to fill the gap, but with much less prudential oversight of the underlying activities than applies to banks.

This is, in many analysts' view, the single most important *unfinished* piece of post-crisis reform: the regulatory perimeter and the credit-intermediation perimeter remain misaligned, and the misalignment has grown rather than shrunk.

## 10.8 Household Balance Sheet

### 10.8.1 Pre-crisis

Households were deeply leveraged into housing. Mortgage debt was a large share of household balance sheets. Home equity was thin (CLTVs of 90%+ on bubble-vintage loans). Consumer debt was also elevated. Households were structurally short housing volatility and short asset prices.

### 10.8.2 Current

- Household debt-to-disposable-income ratio: ~96% vs. 132% pre-crisis peak.
- Mortgage debt service ratio: 4.0% of disposable income vs. 6.5% pre-crisis peak.
- Total household debt service ratio (mortgages plus consumer): 9.7% vs. 13.2% pre-crisis peak.
- Home equity: ~$30 trillion vs. ~$13 trillion pre-crisis (in nominal terms; LTV-equivalent is far lower).
- Net worth: at record highs, supported by both housing equity and equity market appreciation.

But:

- Consumer credit delinquencies are rising.
- Affordability for new homebuyers is severely strained.
- The lock-in effect masks aggregate strength with distributional fragility.
- Inequality of wealth distribution is wider than pre-crisis: the median household balance sheet has improved less than the aggregate metrics suggest.

### 10.8.3 Assessment

The U.S. household balance sheet in aggregate is materially stronger than pre-crisis. This is one of the most consequential and underappreciated changes. A 2008-style household-debt-driven downturn — which depended on millions of households defaulting on mortgages — is not the current set-up. The system's resilience to a housing-market shock is materially higher.

However, the strength is concentrated in older, asset-rich households. Younger, lower-income households face affordability stress and rising consumer debt burdens. The wealth gap is wider; the political economy of housing remains contested.

## 10.9 Concentration of Risk

### 10.9.1 Pre-crisis

Risk was concentrated at a relatively small number of dealer banks (Bear, Lehman, Merrill, Goldman, Morgan Stanley, Citigroup, JPMorgan) and one large insurance company (AIG). Each of these institutions, individually, was systemically important; their interconnection produced the cascading failure dynamic.

### 10.9.2 Current

Risk is concentrated:

- At the largest banks (now even larger after the post-crisis consolidation: JPMorgan absorbed Bear and WaMu; Bank of America absorbed Countrywide and Merrill; Wells Fargo absorbed Wachovia).
- At the major CCPs (LCH, CME, ICE).
- At the major non-bank credit managers (Apollo, Blackstone, Blue Owl, Ares, KKR).
- At the GSEs (in conservatorship).
- At the Federal Reserve (massive MBS and Treasury holdings).
- At the major investment management firms holding "passive" assets (BlackRock $11+ trillion, Vanguard $9+ trillion, State Street $4+ trillion).

The largest U.S. bank (JPMorgan) is now larger than the entire investment banking sector was pre-crisis. The largest investment manager (BlackRock) has a financial-system role unprecedented in history. The Federal Reserve's balance sheet at $7+ trillion is several multiples of its pre-crisis size.

### 10.9.3 Assessment

The largest financial entities are larger and more concentrated than pre-crisis. Post-crisis reforms increased capital, supervision, and resolution planning at these institutions but did not reduce their size or concentration. The "too big to fail" problem was *acknowledged* by Dodd-Frank but not solved; the institutions have become larger and more important to the system than they were in 2008.

This is a deeply uncomfortable conclusion. It implies that the implicit federal commitment to these institutions is, if anything, more binding than pre-crisis.

## 10.10 Transparency and Disclosure

### 10.10.1 Pre-crisis

Disclosure was poor across many parts of the system. Loan-level data on subprime MBS was unavailable to many investors. CDO collateral was opaque. Synthetic exposures were not aggregated or reportable. Risk reports relied on proprietary models with limited supervisory visibility.

### 10.10.2 Current

- **SEC Regulation AB II**: loan-level data required for many ABS.
- **Swap reporting**: nearly all swaps reportable to swap data repositories.
- **Form PF, Form ADV-IA**: hedge fund and private fund disclosure to the SEC.
- **Stress test disclosure**: CCAR results published.
- **G-SIB disclosure**: enhanced disclosure of systemic-importance metrics.
- **Total Loss-Absorbing Capacity (TLAC)** and Minimum Requirement for own funds and Eligible Liabilities (MREL) disclosure.

But:

- Private credit valuations are not publicly disclosed.
- Insurance company alternative allocations are disclosed only annually and at low granularity.
- Bilateral derivatives outside the cleared regime have limited public reporting.
- Open-ended fund portfolio holdings are reported monthly or quarterly, with delays.
- Many of the most important risks (e.g., concentration in specific borrowers across multiple credit relationships) require synthetic aggregation that is not available.

### 10.10.3 Assessment

Transparency has improved substantially at the regulatory level — supervisors have far better visibility into the financial system than pre-crisis. Public transparency has improved less, with significant opacity remaining at non-bank intermediaries and in bilateral OTC arrangements.

A separate consideration: the post-crisis transparency push may have produced *more* fragility in some respects, by making bad news propagate faster. The 2023 SVB run was a transparency-accelerated event. Whether more transparency is always better is a genuinely contested policy question.

## 10.11 Regulatory Oversight

### 10.11.1 Pre-crisis

Regulatory authority was fragmented and gaps existed:
- The OTS supervised AIG; the OTS was abolished by Dodd-Frank for cause.
- The SEC supervised investment banks; the CSE program was inadequate.
- Derivatives markets were largely unregulated post-2000 CFMA.
- No regulator had authority over the system as a whole.

### 10.11.2 Current

- The FSOC coordinates macroprudential policy.
- The Federal Reserve has broad supervisory authority over G-SIBs and substantial non-bank SIFIs.
- The CFTC and SEC have clear derivatives jurisdiction.
- The OFR provides macroprudential analytics.
- The FDIC has resolution authority under OLA.

But:

- The political economy of non-bank designation has constrained FSOC's ability to bring asset managers, hedge funds, and other non-banks within enhanced supervision.
- Title II resolution authority has not been used at a major institution.
- The CFPB has faced sustained political opposition.

### 10.11.3 Assessment

Regulatory architecture is substantially improved. Whether the architecture has the political support to be deployed in stress is uncertain. The 2023 SVB experience showed that authorities can act decisively when needed, but it also showed that the standard pre-crisis tools (FDIC resolution) were insufficient and ad hoc systemic risk exception was required.

## 10.12 Crisis Response Capacity

### 10.12.1 Pre-crisis

The Federal Reserve had limited Section 13(3) authority. Treasury had no comprehensive crisis response framework. FDIC had standard resolution authority for insured depositories but no broader powers. The post-crisis legislative and Fed responses (TARP, AMLF, CPFF, etc.) were assembled ad hoc.

### 10.12.2 Current

The Federal Reserve has:

- Established crisis response tools that can be quickly redeployed (Standing Repo Facility, FIMA repo, modified discount window).
- Demonstrated willingness to lend extensively in stress (2020 COVID; 2023 BTFP).
- Section 13(3) authority constrained but available with Treasury concurrence.

Treasury has TARP-like authority that could be re-invoked with Congressional support, plus Exchange Stabilization Fund authority.

FDIC has Orderly Liquidation Authority that, if used, allows for resolution of a SIFI outside bankruptcy.

But:

- The implicit federal balance sheet is large but bounded. U.S. federal debt-to-GDP at 124% reduces the political and economic optionality for another round of large fiscal interventions.
- Inflation experience of 2021–2024 has reduced central bank willingness to use unlimited monetary accommodation.
- Political polarization has reduced the speed at which Congressional action can be obtained.
- Cross-border coordination is more difficult in a geopolitically fragmented environment.

### 10.12.3 Assessment

Crisis response capacity is, in technical terms, substantially better than pre-crisis. In political-economy and balance-sheet terms, it is constrained. The Federal Reserve can do more, but the political tolerance for further central bank balance sheet expansion is lower than in 2008–2010. Fiscal capacity is reduced.

The 2020 and 2023 episodes were both managed successfully but each at significant political cost. A future crisis in a less coordinated environment could face material constraints.

## 10.13 Cross-Border Dynamics

### 10.13.1 Pre-crisis

The crisis was global in scope. European banks (UBS, Deutsche Bank, RBS, Société Générale, BNP Paribas) held substantial U.S. subprime exposure through their U.S. operations and SIV conduits. Cross-border dollar funding stress was managed through Fed swap lines extended to ECB, BoE, BoJ, SNB, and Bank of Canada.

### 10.13.2 Current

- Federal Reserve swap lines remain available and have been broadened.
- The FIMA repo facility provides another dollar liquidity channel.
- European bank balance sheets have improved (though Italian and German banks remain weaker than U.S. peers).
- Chinese financial system is much larger than pre-crisis but largely outside the U.S.-led financial system.
- Cross-border capital flows are more contested and subject to sanctions/restrictions.

### 10.13.3 Assessment

Cross-border crisis tools are stronger than pre-crisis. The geopolitical environment is more fragmented, which makes coordination during crisis more difficult. The dollar's role as global reserve currency has been mildly eroded but remains dominant.

## 10.14 Summary Comparison Table

| Securitization disclosure | Poor | Better | **Better** |

| Household balance sheet | Stressed | Improved | **Better** |

| Shadow banking size | ~$30tn | ~$65tn (narrow NBFI) | **Worse** |

| Sovereign debt levels | 64% GDP (U.S.) | 124% GDP (U.S.) | **Worse** |
| Open-ended bond fund liquidity mismatch | Moderate | Material | **Worse** |

| CCP concentration risk | Low | High | **Worse / new** |

| Geopolitical financial stress | Modest | Elevated | **New** |

| Climate-related financial risk | Marginal awareness | Active supervisory concern | **New** |
| AI-driven financial activity | Marginal | Growing | **New** |
| Treasury market liquidity | Robust | Structurally weaker | **Worse** |
| Commercial real estate (office) | Cyclical | Structural decline | **Worse** |
| Leveraged loan covenant quality | Restrictive | Cov-lite dominant | **Worse** |
| Stress testing | Did not exist | Standard | **Better** |

The net assessment: the post-2008 reforms have substantially improved the **core** of the financial system (the major banks, the agency mortgage market, the standardized derivatives market) and have substantially **expanded the perimeter** of activities that fall outside primary prudential supervision (private credit, non-bank intermediaries, CCPs, certain shadow banking activities, sovereign debt levels).

The system is, on net, more resistant to a 2008-style crisis originating in subprime mortgage credit. It is potentially less resistant to crises originating in different parts of the system — particularly in non-bank credit intermediation, sovereign debt, or scenarios involving simultaneous stress across multiple sectors that constrain the central bank's optionality.

The deepest unresolved question is whether the implicit federal backstop, which has expanded with each crisis, is durable. The fiscal cost of the 2008 and 2020 interventions was modest in cash terms but the cumulative federal commitments are now much larger. A future crisis on the scale of 2008–2009 in a less politically supportive environment, with U.S. debt-to-GDP starting at 124%, would face genuinely binding constraints. The system's stability depends, more than is usually acknowledged, on the credibility of an implicit guarantee whose actual deployment in extremis has not been tested under those conditions.

## 10.15 The Three-Bucket Framework

Synthesizing the comparison:

**Bucket 1 — What got fixed.**
- G-SIB capitalization
- Mortgage underwriting in the agency channel
- Standardized OTC derivatives clearing
- Bank liquidity standards (LCR/NSFR)
- Pre-crisis structured products (synthetic CDOs, CDO-squareds)
- Resolution authority (OLA, TLAC, MREL)
- Stress testing
- Issuer-paid rating agency conflicts (partially)

**Bucket 2 — What got shifted.**
- Credit intermediation: from banks to private credit and non-banks
- Securitization: from private label to GSE
- Mortgage origination: from banks to non-banks
- Leverage: from investment bank balance sheets to fund vehicles
- Counterparty risk: from bilateral to CCP
- Funding fragility: from dealer balance sheets to open-ended funds
- Implicit guarantees: from banks to expanded perimeter

**Bucket 3 — What's genuinely new.**
- Sovereign debt overhang
- CCP concentration as systemic point
- ETF and passive investment scale
- Cryptocurrency / stablecoin overlay
- AI-driven trading and credit decisioning
- Climate-related financial risk
- Cyber-financial risk
- Geopolitical/sanctions fragmentation of the dollar system
- The Federal Reserve as primary market maker for fixed income broadly

The structure of the next crisis, whenever it comes, will draw on elements from buckets 2 and 3. The lessons of 2008 are applied; the configurations that produce the next crisis will be different. Section 11 attempts a final synthesis.
